# Concept Basis — Heap / Priority Queue

> [!abstract] Minimal spanning set for heaps. One entry per **new idea you have to learn**. Variations live in the exclusions table with the entry each collapses into. Cross-listed entries (↗) are developed further elsewhere but belong here too.

## What a heap actually gives you, and what it does not

> [!tip] Every idea in this file is a consequence of one sentence: **a heap gives you the extreme element in `O(log n)` and tells you nothing else.**
>
> It cannot give you the second element without removing the first. It cannot delete an arbitrary element. It cannot answer a range query. It has no order beyond the root. Each of those limitations, worked around, is an entry below — lazy deletion exists because of the deletion limit, two heaps exist because of the "only one extreme" limit, and the size-`k` inversion exists because you cannot ask for the `k`-th directly.
>
> The corollary worth internalising: **if you find yourself sorting inside a loop, you wanted a heap.** If you find yourself needing the middle, you wanted two heaps or a balanced BST.

## Mechanism axes

Enumerated before any problem was named.

| Axis | Values |
|---|---|
| **What is ordered** | the raw value · a derived key (frequency, distance, ratio) · a **marginal gain** · a tuple with an explicit tie-break · an event time |
| **Heap size** | fixed at `k` · one slot per source · unbounded · two heaps split at a median |
| **What triggers a pop** | overflow past `k` · time advancing · budget exhausted · a dependency becoming satisfied · every step of a merge |
| **Removal discipline** | eager, top only · **lazy** (mark stale, purge on surfacing) · indexed decrease-key |
| **Where the input comes from** | a fixed collection · a live stream · **generated by the heap's own output** · sweep-line events |
| **Online or offline** | pure streaming · sort first then heap · both at once |
| **Where the answer is read** | the top · the heap's *size* · the accumulated sequence of pops · whatever survives at the end |
| **What happens to what leaves** | **discarded** (top-`k` evicts losers permanently) · **emitted** (a reordering buffer outputs everything, in order) · **transferred** to a second structure |
| **Assumption that breaks** | no arbitrary delete · no order below the root · no range queries · `n` pushes is `O(n log n)` but heapify is `O(n)` |

## H1 · Mechanics

Skip these and you will write correct but needlessly slow heap code, and you will get the Dijkstra follow-up question wrong.

| # | Problem | Source | The new idea |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Build a heap in `O(n)` | *classic — the `heapify` in every stdlib* | **Bottom-up sift-down beats `n` pushes.** Start at the last internal node and sift down; the cost telescopes to `O(n)`, not `O(n log n)`, because most nodes are near the bottom and barely move. Heapsort falls out of the same primitive: repeatedly swap the root to the end and re-sift, giving in-place `O(n log n)` with `O(1)` extra space. The one place you should know *why* the bound holds, since it is a standard follow-up. |
| 2 | Lazy deletion vs. indexed decrease-key | *classic — the two ways to write Dijkstra* | **A heap cannot delete an arbitrary element, and there are exactly two workarounds.** Either push a duplicate and discard stale entries when they surface (simple, heap grows to `O(E)`), or maintain a position index so you can decrease a key in place (`O(V)` memory, harder to write). Interviews almost always want the lazy version — but knowing the tradeoff exists is what the question is testing. This axis reappears in #12, #22 and #23. |

## H2 · Top-K — the size-`k` heap

> [!tip] The recognition signal is "the `k` best," where `k` is much smaller than `n`. The answer is always a heap of size `k`, and it is always **inverted** relative to what you would guess.

| # | Problem | Source | The new idea |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3 | Kth Largest Element in an Array | LC **215** | **The inversion.** To keep the `k` largest you need a **min**-heap, because the thing you must be able to evict cheaply is the smallest of your survivors. Getting this backwards is the single most common heap mistake. Cost is `O(n log k)`, and the root is the answer. Quickselect is the `O(n)`-average alternative worth naming. |
| 4 | Top K Frequent Elements | LC **347** | **Order by a derived key.** Count first, then heap on the count while carrying the element along. Two things generalise: the heap never has to hold the thing you are ranking, and **bucket sort by count is `O(n)`** here because frequencies are bounded by `n` — a heap is not always the right answer even when it works. |
| 5 | Kth Largest Element in a Stream | LC **703** | **Online top-k.** The size-`k` heap survives insertion, which is the thing sorting cannot do. This is the entry that justifies the whole family: with a fixed input you could just sort, so the heap only truly earns its place when data arrives over time. |
| 6 | Maximum Performance of a Team | LC **1383** | **Sort on one criterion, heap on the other.** Two dimensions to optimise, so fix one by iterating in sorted order — then every prefix has a known worst value of that dimension, and a size-`k` heap maintains the best of the second. The template for every "maximise `min(a) × sum(b)`" problem. |
| 24 | Sort a nearly-sorted (`k`-sorted) array | *classic — every element is at most `k` places from home* | **A bounded heap as a reordering buffer, which emits rather than discards.** Hold `k+1` elements; the minimum among them is provably the next element of the sorted output, so pop it, emit it, and push the next input. `O(n log k)` time and `O(k)` space, and it streams — you never hold the array. The contrast with #3 is the point and is why this is not a variation: **top-`k` throws the loser away, a reordering buffer eventually emits everything.** Same structure, opposite contract, and the streaming property is what makes it the standard answer for merging sorted chunks off disk. |

## H3 · K-way merge & lazy generation

| # | Problem | Source | The new idea |
|---|---|---|---|
| 7 | Merge k Sorted Lists | LC **23** | **One candidate per source.** The heap holds `k` items — the current head of each list — so its size is the *number of sources*, unrelated to the answer's size. Pop the min, then push that source's successor. Contrast H2, where `k` was the answer size. |
| 8 | Find K Pairs with Smallest Sums | LC **373** | **Traverse a sorted grid that is never built.** Conceptually there is an `n × m` matrix of pair sums, sorted along both axes. Push only the neighbours of what you pop, and you walk the smallest `k` cells while materialising almost nothing. The move is *generating candidates lazily instead of enumerating them*. |
| 9 | Ugly Number II | LC **264** | **The heap feeds itself.** Output becomes input: pop the smallest, multiply it by each factor, push the results back. The sequence has no precomputed source at all. Requires deduplication, which is where the `seen` set enters. |
| 10 | Smallest Range Covering Elements from K Lists | LC **632** | **A sliding window across `k` sorted lists at once.** The heap supplies the current minimum and you track the maximum separately, so popping the min is what advances the window's left edge. Fuses the merge machinery of #7 with two-pointer thinking. |

## H4 · Two heaps

| # | Problem | Source | The new idea |
|---|---|---|---|
| 11 | Find Median from Data Stream | LC **295** | **Split the data at a point and keep a heap on each side** — a max-heap for the lower half facing a min-heap for the upper. Both medians sit at the two roots. The whole difficulty is the rebalancing invariant: sizes differ by at most one, and every insert may need one transfer across. |
| 12 | Sliding Window Median | LC **480** | **Lazy deletion.** Elements must now leave from the *middle*, which a heap cannot do. So you mark them dead in a hash map and purge only when they reach a root, keeping a separate count of live elements for the balance check. The most transferable idea in this file — it is also how you do #23 and how you would do sliding-window maximum without a deque ([[Stack and Queue]] #21). |
| 13 | IPO | LC **502** | **Two structures with a one-way flow between them.** Projects sit in a list sorted by capital; as your budget grows they migrate into a max-heap keyed by profit, and you always take that heap's root. The pattern is "**unlock as a resource grows**" — the second structure holds everything currently affordable, and it only ever gains members. |

## H5 · Scheduling & simulation

| # | Problem | Source | The new idea |
|---|---|---|---|
| 14 | Meeting Rooms II | LC **253** | **The heap's `size` is the answer.** Sort by start, push each end time, pop everything that has already finished. What the heap tracks is *how many things are live right now*, and you never read its contents. First problem where the heap is a counter, not a selector. |
| 15 | Task Scheduler | LC **621** | **A cooldown park beside the heap.** Pop the most frequent, then hold it in a waiting area until its cooldown expires before it may re-enter. Two containers with a *time-triggered* transfer, where #13's transfer was budget-triggered. |
| 16 | Single-Threaded CPU | LC **1834** | **Offline sort meets online heap.** Sort by availability time to decide *when* something becomes a candidate; heap by priority to decide *which* candidate to run. The clock jumps forward when the heap empties. Separating "eligible" from "chosen" is the transferable move. |
| 17 | Maximum Number of Events That Can Be Attended | LC **1353** | **Heap keyed by deadline.** Sweep day by day, admit every event that has started, and greedily attend the one that expires soonest. The exchange argument — taking the tightest deadline first is never worse — is the actual content, and it is a proof you should be able to give aloud. |

## H6 · Greedy with regret

> [!tip] The defining move of this family: **make a greedy choice, and undo it later when it turns out to be wrong.** A plain greedy commits; a regret greedy keeps its past decisions in a heap so the worst of them stays cheap to revoke.

| # | Problem | Source | The new idea |
|---|---|---|---|
| 18 | Course Schedule III | LC **630** | **Retroactive eviction.** Take every course as you meet it, and the moment you overrun the deadline, drop the longest course you have already taken — including, possibly, the one you just added. A max-heap of accepted durations makes that eviction `O(log n)`. Astonishing the first time: you commit to things specifically so you can un-commit later. |
| 19 | Maximum Average Pass Ratio | LC **1792** | **Rank by marginal gain, not by value.** The heap is ordered by how much the ratio would *improve* if this class got the next student, which is a derived quantity that changes after every pop. The general shape of "distribute `k` units of a resource optimally" — and the trap is that ordering by current value gives the wrong answer. |

## H7 · Structural greedy

| # | Problem | Source | The new idea |
|---|---|---|---|
| 20 | Minimum Cost to Connect Sticks | LC **1167** | **Huffman.** Repeatedly merge the two smallest and push the result back. Cost accumulates over merges, and the reason it is optimal is that the smallest items must sit deepest in the merge tree. Same algorithm as Huffman coding; recognising an unfamiliar problem as this shape is the skill. |
| 21 | Minimize Deviation in Array | LC **1675** | **Normalise so every move goes one way.** Operations here are "double an odd" and "halve an even," which is a mess in both directions — so pre-multiply every odd number once, and now only halving remains. A monotone process can be driven by a max-heap: shrink the maximum, record the spread, repeat until the max is odd. The lesson is the preprocessing that makes the heap applicable at all. |

## H8 · Heap-ordered traversal

Here the heap stops being a selector over a collection and becomes the **frontier of a search**.

| # | Problem | Source | The new idea |
|---|---|---|---|
| 22 | Trapping Rain Water II | LC **407** | **Expand inward from the boundary, always from the lowest wall.** In 2D there is no left-max/right-max decomposition to exploit, so instead you process the frontier in increasing height order — whatever you reach is bounded by the lowest wall you crossed. This is Dijkstra with "water level" as the distance. The strongest single problem in this file. |
| 23 | The Skyline Problem | LC **218** | **Sweep-line events with a lazily-purged max-heap.** Sort building edges into events, keep active heights in a heap, and emit a keypoint whenever the maximum changes. Buildings end in an order unrelated to their heights, so arbitrary removal is needed — hence #12's lazy deletion, in its natural home. |

## Self-audit

**Borderline calls, and which way I went**

- **Furthest Building You Can Reach (LC 1642) excluded into #18.** The least comfortable call in this file. Course Schedule III drops a past choice entirely; LC 1642 *downgrades* one resource into another. Under a strict reading both are "revise a past greedy decision with a heap," so they merge — but the downgrade framing is different enough that ==you should split them if #18 does not transfer to 1642 when you try it==.
- **Minimum Number of Refueling Stops cross-listed rather than made native.** It is arguably the cleanest regret-greedy problem there is and could headline H6. Left as ↗ because the *reason* it matters is the DP dimension it eliminates, which is developed properly in the DP file.
- **Heapify (#1) as an entry at all.** It is a fact rather than a problem-solving move. Kept because it is asked directly and because heapsort depends on it.
- **Lazy deletion (#2) stated in mechanics and then re-taught in #12.** Deliberate duplication — you will not absorb it as an abstraction, only through Sliding Window Median.
- **Meeting Rooms II (#14) versus a difference array.** Kept as a heap entry, though for the pure counting version a difference array is strictly better. The heap earns its place only when you must know *which* resources are active, not merely how many.
- **Two heaps (#11) and lazy deletion (#12) kept split.** #11 is a balance invariant; #12 is a deletion workaround. They co-occur constantly but are independent ideas — you need #12 for #23, which has no median in it at all.

**Step 4B — reverse sweep**

Thirty plain-language descriptions were navigated against the family headings. One failure:

- **"Each element is at most `k` positions from where it belongs — sort it cheaply"** landed nowhere. That is #24, now in H2. The axis it exposed is **what happens to what leaves the heap**: top-`k` discards the loser permanently, a reordering buffer emits everything eventually, and a scheduling problem transfers it to a second structure. My axes covered where the *answer* is read but never what happens to the *rejects*, which is exactly what separates #3 from #24 despite identical code.

Also confirmed by the sweep: the missing sorted-container topic is real. **"I keep inserting and need the rank so far"** navigates to nothing in any of the six files, which is now the third independent confirmation.

**What I am uncertain about**

- **Interval heaps and double-ended priority queues** (`O(1)` access to both min and max in one structure) — excluded as out of scope. Two separate heaps is what interviews want. Reasonable confidence.
- **Fibonacci and pairing heaps** — excluded as competitive-programming and theory scope. High confidence.
- **Soft heaps, `d`-ary heaps** — excluded. `d`-ary is worth one sentence if a cache-locality question ever comes up, which it will not.
- **Median maintenance via a balanced BST or ordered multiset** — the alternative to #11 and #12, and genuinely the better tool in C++ where `multiset` exists. Belongs to a Sorted-Container basis that does not exist yet. **This is the most likely real gap in the file.**
- **The scheduling family (H5) is the one most at risk of a wrong merge.** Five near-identical-looking problems collapsed into four entries; if any exclusion is wrong it is in there.
- **`k`-way merge with an indexed heap for external sorting** — real, but a systems-design question rather than a DSA one.

**Completeness confidence: ~92%.** Slightly below Graphs and DP, for one specific reason: the heap is a *tool* rather than a topic, so its boundary with greedy, intervals and graphs is genuinely fuzzy rather than merely unclear. The cross-listing rule helps a great deal here — under the old ownership rule this file would have lost Dijkstra, Prim and the refuelling problem, which is roughly a third of why anyone learns heaps at all.
